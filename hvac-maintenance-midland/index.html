What should HVAC maintenance cover?

Maintenance is a check of the equipment and how it operates, not just a quick look at the thermostat. For an air conditioner, a visit commonly includes reviewing the filter, thermostat settings, accessible electrical connections, condensate drain, indoor and outdoor equipment, and system operation.

The exact work depends on the system, its condition, and what the manufacturer recommends. A technician should tell you what was inspected, note any concerns, and separate routine service from repairs that need your approval. If a part needs replacement or a deeper cleaning is recommended, ask what the work involves before authorizing it.

What does a local technician check during the visit?

A careful check looks at the whole cooling setup, including airflow, drainage, and visible signs of wear. In Midland, it's sensible to book before the hottest part of the season if you can, since a system problem is harder to put off when cooling is needed every day.

  • Filter condition and whether the installed filter is the right size and type.
  • Thermostat operation, cooling response, and accessible system controls.
  • Outdoor unit condition, including visible debris or damage that could restrict airflow.
  • Condensate drain and pan for signs of blockage, standing water, or leakage.
  • Accessible wiring and electrical components for visible wear or loose connections.
  • System operation and airflow, with any unusual sounds or performance concerns recorded.

Not every maintenance visit includes coil cleaning, refrigerant work, or repairs. Those tasks depend on what the technician finds, the equipment, and the agreed scope. Ask which checks are included in the scheduled visit and whether additional work would require a separate approval.

What affects the cost and appointment timing?

The price depends on the agreed scope, the number and type of systems, how accessible the equipment is, and its condition. A standard preventive check is different from troubleshooting a system that is not cooling, clearing a blocked drain, cleaning heavily soiled components, or replacing a part. Ask for the maintenance charge and approval process before the appointment, and request a separate explanation for any additional work.

Timing can depend on the season, the provider's schedule, the equipment location, and whether the visit stays within routine maintenance. Booking ahead of a high-demand stretch can give you more flexibility. If the system has stopped working or is leaking, tell the provider when you call so the appointment can be scheduled as a service concern rather than treated as routine upkeep.

What should you prepare, and what mistakes should you avoid?

A little information helps the technician understand the system and spend the visit on useful checks. You don't need to diagnose the equipment yourself; share what you've noticed and leave testing or repair work to a qualified professional.

  • Note when the issue started and whether it happens all the time or only under certain conditions.
  • Share any recent changes, such as unusual noises, weak airflow, water near the indoor unit, or rooms that feel different from usual.
  • Clear ordinary access to the indoor and outdoor equipment, without opening panels or moving anything unsafe.
  • Have the system model information available if it's easy to find, but don't remove equipment covers to look for it.
  • Avoid assuming that a filter change alone counts as a full system inspection.
  • Don't approve a repair you don't understand. Ask what was found, what the proposed work addresses, and whether the system can safely wait.

When should you call a professional instead of waiting for maintenance?

Book service promptly if the system stops cooling, repeatedly shuts off, makes a new grinding or buzzing sound, leaks water, or produces a burning smell. A maintenance appointment can uncover issues, but an active fault may need troubleshooting rather than a routine check.

If you notice a burning smell or suspect an electrical problem, stop using the system if it's safe to do so and contact a professional. For water around equipment, avoid touching electrical parts and describe the leak when you call. Don't remove access panels or attempt repairs that require working with electrical components or refrigerant.

Questions to ask before you hire

  • What inspection and cleaning tasks are included in the maintenance visit?
  • Will the technician check both the indoor and outdoor parts of my system?
  • Do you work on my equipment type and system configuration?
  • What is the maintenance charge, and what could lead to additional charges?
  • Will you explain any recommended repair and get my approval before doing extra work?
  • Do you provide a summary of what was checked and what needs attention?
  • How soon can you schedule the visit, and what should I do if the system stops working before then?
  • Are there any local permit or code requirements for work beyond routine maintenance?

Clear answers help you compare providers on the actual service, rather than on a vague promise of a tune-up. If the company recommends work outside the original appointment, ask for the reason and scope before deciding.
